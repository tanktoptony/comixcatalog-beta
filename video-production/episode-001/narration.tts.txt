Episode one. Where to start reading X-Men... without losing your mind.

A few years ago, I walked into Graham Crackers Comics in Chicago, as a grown adult, and asked the guy behind the counter the most embarrassing question you can ask in a comic shop. [sighs] Where do I start with X-Men? Because X-Men is sixty years of comics. A dozen books with X-Men in the title. And reading orders online that look like tax forms. He didn't hand me a reading order. He handed me one book. And it worked.

So that's what this video is. Not a reading order. Five books. One shelf. [excited] Let's go.

Quick background. I was eight years old, growing up in Chicagoland, in 1992, when X-Men: The Animated Series came on... and flipped a switch in my brain. That theme song still lives rent free, and it gets me pumped every single time.

My best friend is the one who got us all into it. The cartoon, the comics, all of it. So he got first pick... and he picked Gambit. [sarcastic] Obviously. Gambit and Wolverine were the coolest guys on the show. And Gambit had the trench coat, the glowing cards, and an accent none of us could understand. He also decided the girl everybody in class had a crush on... was Rogue. [laughs] Kids.

I got Colossus. And I felt cheated. Big Russian guy, turns into steel, says about four words an episode... but he was mine. So I stuck with him. And somewhere along the way, I actually started to care about him. Strongest guy in the room... and he'd rather be home painting.

I don't think I'm the only one this happened to. A lot of us didn't pick our X-Man. We got handed one, and then we got attached. That's how I got into X-Men. Not through a reading order. Through a character. Hold on to that. I'm gonna come back to it in a minute.

The obvious move is to start at the beginning. X-Men number one. 1963. It matters historically. Stan Lee and Jack Kirby. The original five. Magneto's first appearance. Honestly? It feels like a different team. But it's good background, and it matters more than you'd think once we get to Hickman. Hold that thought, too.

But historically important and a good first read... are not the same thing. The storytelling has changed a lot in sixty years. And you don't need to do homework to enjoy this stuff. So treat it like a tasting menu. Five stories, from different eras. If one grabs you, follow it. If one doesn't, skip it. [chuckles] Nobody's checking.

Number one. The Dark Phoenix Saga. Uncanny X-Men one twenty-nine through one thirty-seven. 1980. Chris Claremont and John Byrne. If you only read one X-Men story... it's probably this one. It's the team at their peak. And it's where a lot of what you know about the X-Men comes from.

Issue one twenty-nine alone introduces Kitty Pryde and Emma Frost. One thirty gives you Dazzler... who's a disco superhero. And it's not as corny as it sounds. But the real story is Jean Grey. She's gained cosmic power. The Hellfire Club messes with her head. And it goes about as well as you'd expect.

What makes it hold up is that it's not just a big fight, like Secret Wars. It's a team, trying to save a friend from herself. And then... one thirty-seven. I'm not gonna spoil it for you. I'll just say it's one of the most famous endings in comics, for a reason.

I'd been through the Phoenix story a bunch of times before I ever read the actual book. The cartoon did it, and honestly, did it really well. The movies have tried it twice... and I'm still waiting for one to get it right. But it didn't matter. Even knowing how it ends, the trade paperback is still the best way to experience it. Team dynamics. Real consequences. And a character arc that people are still adapting forty years later. If you start anywhere... start here.

Number two. Days of Future Past. Uncanny X-Men one forty-one and one forty-two. Short and sweet. Two issues. That covers the whole pitch. A future where the Sentinels won, and most of the X-Men are dead, or in camps. Kitty's mind gets sent back to the present to stop it from happening. It's short. It's dark. And it's the blueprint for every stop-the-bad-future story the X-Men have done since. Including the movie... and the cartoon episode you probably saw first.

Number three. Ex-cutioner's Song. This one's for the kids who grew up on the cartoon. Ninety-two into ninety-three. A crossover across four X-books. Professor X gets shot, Cable's the main suspect, and it turns into a whole mess with Strife and Apocalypse. [excited] It is the most nineties thing you will ever read. Everybody's squinting. Everybody has pouches. [laughs] It's pretty rad. If the cartoon is your X-Men, this is the comic that feels like it. Fair warning... it is messy. It jumps across four different books, so grab the trade paperback. Puts everything in order for you.

Number four. God Loves, Man Kills. 1982. Chris Claremont and Brent Anderson. A standalone graphic novel. No continuity homework. You pick it up, you read it, you're done. A preacher named William Stryker starts a crusade against mutants, and the X-Men end up working with Magneto to stop him. This is the X-Men at their most direct about what they've always been about. Fear. Prejudice. People being hated for what they are. It's also the backbone of the second X-Men movie, if that's your entry point. It's heavy. But it's the one that shows you why these stories still matter.

Number five. House of M. 2005. The legendary Brian Michael Bendis, and Olivier Kwah-pel. This is the book the guy at Graham Crackers handed me. The Scarlet Witch rewrites reality. Everybody wakes up in a world where they got what they wanted... and a few of them start to realize something's wrong. It's an event book, so it's big, and loud, and it touches the Avengers too. But it reads clean, and it was the perfect way back in for me as a young adult. And the ending changed the X-Men for about a decade. Three words. You'll know them when you see them.

Once you've got a few of these under your belt, then read House of X and Powers of X. 2019. Jonathan Hickman. It's twelve issues that flip decades of X-Men history on its head. Remember X-Men number one? This is where Xavier's choices, going all the way back to the beginning, start catching up with him. It's brilliant. And it's also... a lot. It lands so much harder when you know who these people are, and what they've been through. So don't start there. Save it. It'll be waiting.

And here's my actual advice. Shop like a kid. When you were ten, you didn't need a reading order. You grabbed whatever cover looked cool, and figured it out. Find a character you like. For me, it was Colossus... and I didn't even pick him. For you, it might be Storm. Then you can follow them. Read their stories. Jump around. Get confused. Look stuff up. That's half the fun.

So, the shelf. Dark Phoenix. Days of Future Past. Ex-cutioner's Song. God Loves, Man Kills. And House of M. And then House of X and Powers of X, when you're ready. Don't turn reading comics into homework. Go pick up a comic... and come back and tell me which one you started with.
