## 02-intro · ON CAMERA, then VO over Tony's own books · ~1:40

P3. This guy has written some of the biggest, most ambitious stories Marvel has published in recent memory. And with the MCU exploring so many of the same enormous concepts, it's not surprising that people are paying attention to his work.

P4. But here's something funny.

P5. I've been collecting comics for most of my life. And for years, I was picking up these strange books called East of West and The Manhattan Projects.

P6. I loved the covers. They were sparse, mysterious, sometimes almost completely devoid of the usual comic-book clutter. And the artwork inside? Especially in The Manhattan Projects? Just completely nuts. It had this weird, almost Garbage Pail Kids quality that I absolutely loved.

P7. And somehow, until pretty recently, I hadn't connected those books with Jonathan Hickman.

P8. So that got me thinking.

P9. A lot of people know Hickman because of Marvel. But how many have explored the bizarre, inventive comics he was making outside of it?

P10. Because if you like what he did with the X-Men, or the enormous interconnected story that led to Secret Wars, there's a whole other side of his work worth discovering.

P11. And that's what I want to talk about today.

[TITLE CARD]

## 03-the-marvel-guy · VO · ~1:50
Spoilers: premise of Secret Wars (2015) only. House of X #2 is named but not explained.

P12. Real quick, the Marvel side, because it matters for everything else.

P13. Hickman took over Fantastic Four in 2009, and that's where you start to see his thing. He plants an idea in one issue and doesn't pay it off for years. Not issues. Years.

P14. Then he did Avengers and New Avengers at the same time, two books side by side. In New Avengers he introduced something called incursions. Two universes collide, and Earth is the point of impact. Either one Earth gets destroyed, or both universes die. And a handful of Marvel's smartest heroes have to decide what they're willing to do about that. It does not go great.

P15. All of it was building to Secret Wars, in 2015, drawn by Esad Ribić. The multiverse ends, and what's left is one patchwork world held together by Doctor Doom. Who is, for the record, having the time of his life.

P16. I loved Secret Wars. Years of setup, two whole Avengers books' worth, and it actually lands. All that stuff you thought was background turns out to be the point.

P17. Then in 2019 he came to the X-Men with House of X and Powers of X. Two six-issue series, one issue a week for twelve weeks, and together they read as one story. Pepe Larraz and R.B. Silva on art.

P18. And House of X #2 has the Moira MacTaggert reveal. If you've read it, you know. If you haven't, I'm not going to be the guy who ruins it. I'll just say it took a character who'd been around since the seventies and made the entire history of the X-Men look different. I was blown away.

P19. That's what gets me about Hickman. He takes a pile of continuity that would scare most writers off, and he makes it feel like it was always going somewhere. And he does it without trashing what the writers before him built.

P20. And yeah, the movies noticed. Doctor Strange in the Multiverse of Madness talks about incursions, which comes straight out of his Avengers books. The next two Avengers movies are Doomsday and Secret Wars. Now, Secret Wars was a Marvel title back in 1984, long before Hickman. So how much of his version ends up on screen? Nobody outside Marvel Studios knows yet. We'll find out.

P21. OK. That's the Marvel guy. Now the books I was buying without knowing he wrote them.

## 04-manhattan-projects · VO, one ON CAMERA beat · ~2:10
Spoilers: none beyond the publisher's own pitch. Issue #1's twist is teased, not told.

P22. The Manhattan Projects. Image Comics, started in 2012. Written by Hickman, drawn by Nick Pitarra, with Ryan Browne on some issues.

P23. You know the real Manhattan Project. World War Two, Los Alamos, the atomic bomb. Now imagine the bomb was the cover story. The thing they let you know about. And the real work going on underneath was way, way weirder.

P24. The cast is real people. Oppenheimer. Einstein. Enrico Fermi. Richard Feynman. Wernher von Braun. General Leslie Groves, who actually ran the project. Except none of them are quite who the history books say they are.

P25. I won't spoil issue one, but there's a twist about Oppenheimer in there that tells you exactly what kind of book you're reading. And it gets stranger. Later on there's a talking Soviet space dog named Laika. There's an artificial intelligence built out of Franklin Roosevelt. I'm not making these up to sound interesting. That's just the book.

P26. And then there's the art, which is what got me in the first place. Nick Pitarra draws faces like he's mad at them. Every character looks a little bit wrong, in the best way. Too many lines, too many teeth, eyes a little too far apart. That's what I mean about Garbage Pail Kids. These are some of the most famous scientists in history, drawn like they came out of a wax pack.

P27. Pitarra has said he's a big Moebius, Geof Darrow, Frank Quitely and Seth Fisher fan, and once you know that, you can see it. Dense detail, weird anatomy, nothing generic.

P28. [ON CAMERA, holding his copy] This is my copy of (your call: which issue). Full disclosure, I bought these as they came out, and years later I couldn't tell you the plot in any real detail. What stuck was the look of it. And the fact that I kept buying it.

P29. It ran 25 issues, 2012 to 2015, plus a four-issue follow-up called The Sun Beyond the Stars.

## 05-east-of-west · VO · ~2:00
Spoilers: setup from issue #1 only. Death's personal motive is teased, not told.

P30. East of West. Also Image, 2013 to 2019, 45 issues. Hickman writing, Nick Dragotta on art, Frank Martin on colors, Rus Wooton on letters.

P31. It's a science fiction western set in an alternate America. In this version of history, the Civil War doesn't end in 1865. It drags on until 1908, when a comet hits Kansas. That's what finally stops the war. The treaty that comes out of it splits the continent into seven nations, and every one of them wants more.

P32. The story picks up in 2064. The Seven Nations are still circling each other. And the Four Horsemen of the Apocalypse are real. Death, War, Famine and Conquest. Except Death has gone off script. He's riding around dressed all in white, with a very personal reason to be angry at the other three. The first issue will tell you what it is.

P33. On top of that there's a prophecy called the Message, pieced together from three sources. A Confederate soldier, a Native American chief, and Chairman Mao. Which is a hell of a group chat. And there's a small circle of elites from each nation, the Chosen, who've decided the end of the world is something they should help along.

P34. The tagline on this series is, "The things that divide us are stronger than the things that unite us." Fun. Totally fictional.

P35. If you liked the data pages in House of X, the maps and charts and timelines, this is that same instinct. Nations, factions inside the nations, a prophecy with rules. It's a book that rewards you for paying attention. Dragotta's art keeps it moving, too. Clean lines, big open panels, gunfights and some very strange technology.

P36. And again, those covers. Giant EAST OF WEST type, one flat color, one image, and a whole lot of white. (your call: add one line about seeing them on the rack, if you remember it.)

P37. Same confession as before. I collected this for years, and I couldn't give you the plot at a party. But here's the good news for anybody starting now. East of West is finished. 45 issues, with a real ending. It got an Eisner nomination for Best Continuing Series in 2014. And Amazon announced a TV show in 2018 that never happened, which honestly might be the most comic-book thing about it.

## 06-deep-cuts · VO · ~1:50
Spoilers: premises only.

P38. Those are the two I owned. But once I looked up what else this guy had done outside Marvel, the list got a lot longer.

P39. Start at the beginning. The Nightly News, 2006. Six issues. His first comic, and he did all of it. Wrote it, drew it, colored it, lettered it. It's about a cult of people whose lives were wrecked by bad news coverage, and they decide the fix is to go after the media. Directly. With a sniper. It got an Eisner nomination for Best Limited Series. On his first book.

P40. You can see the graphic designer in it. Before comics, Hickman studied architecture and worked in advertising and design, and his pages look like it. Diagrams, text blocks, information laid out like a poster.

P41. Then Pax Romana. Four issues, 2007 into 2008, also written and drawn by him. In the future, the Vatican gets its hands on time travel. And the plan is to send an army back to the year 312, the time of Constantine, and fix history in the Church's favor. Five thousand men. What could go wrong. A lot of it is told through transcripts and maps and diagrams, which, if you've read House of X, should sound familiar.

P42. And The Black Monday Murders, 2016, drawn by Tomm Coker. Black Monday is the 1987 stock market crash. The idea is that the big money families are basically schools of magic. Wealth isn't just power, it's actual power. There's a detective, Theodore Dumas, working a murder, and he starts to figure out that finance runs on something a lot older than spreadsheets.

P43. One heads-up: it's eight issues and it's been on hiatus since 2018. It doesn't have an ending yet. Go in knowing that.

P44. And there's more. Transhuman. A Red Mass for Mars. The Red Wing. Secret. The Dying and the Dead. Decorum. The guy does not sit still.

## 07-the-thread · VO · ~1:10

P45. So what ties all this together? Here's my take.

P46. Look at the list. The news media. The Catholic Church. The atomic bomb program. The United States itself. Wall Street. Almost every one of these books takes a big, real institution and asks, what if the people running it were up to something way stranger than you think?

P47. Hickman loves a secret society. He loves a system with rules, and a chart that explains the rules, and then somebody who breaks them.

P48. And that's the same guy who built Krakoa. A mutant nation with its own laws, its own government, its own language. A secret council of heroes deciding which worlds get to live. He was doing that in his own books first, without any Marvel history to lean on. When he finally got the keys to the X-Men, he already knew exactly how to build a world.

## 08-where-to-start · VO · ~1:00

P49. So, where do you start?

P50. If you loved House of X or Secret Wars and you want a story that actually finishes, East of West, Volume One, The Promise. That's issues one through five.

P51. If you want weird and funny and kind of gross, The Manhattan Projects, Volume One, Science Bad. Also issues one through five.

P52. If you want to see where it all started, The Nightly News. Six issues, one book.

P53. Pax Romana if you want something short and strange.

P54. And The Black Monday Murders if you're OK with a great story that might never finish.

P55. And honestly? Check your long boxes first. You might already own some of these. I did.

P57. That's one of my favorite things about collecting. Sometimes the discovery isn't at the shop. It's already in your long box.

P58. (your call: only if true) So I'm going back through them.
