## 02-origin · ON CAMERA, then VO · ~1:30

P6. But before I get into that, I should probably explain why I started building ComixCatalog in the first place.

P7. A few years ago, during COVID, I was living with a roommate who was constantly buying records on Discogs.

P8. I was familiar with the website, but once he actually showed me how he used it, I started getting a little obsessed with the idea.

P9. He could catalog his records, find specific pressings, track approximate values, and buy from sellers all over the world. He could hunt for deals without having to guess what everything was worth.

P10. And I remember thinking about my own comic collection.

P11. Specifically, my Coheed and Cambria comics.

P12. I had books I wanted to find, runs I wanted to finish, and gaps in my collection. Why couldn't I just open a website, find the exact comic I needed, compare listings, and buy it?

P13. I was just starting my master's degree in software engineering, and somewhere in there, the idea started taking shape.

P14. What if somebody made Discogs for comics?

P15. And what if that somebody was me?

P16. Fast-forward to my friend Corey handing me two enormous boxes of comics.

P17. Suddenly I had a pretty good way to find out whether the thing I'd been building actually worked.

## 03-why-discogs-works · VO · ~1:20

P18. If you've never used Discogs, here's the short version. Every record has its own page. And not just the album. The specific pressing. The country, the label, the year, the catalog number. If you own it, you click a button and it's in your collection. If you want it, there are sellers listed right there.

P19. And the whole thing is built by the people who use it. Collectors adding what they own.

P20. Here's something I didn't know until I was researching this video. Discogs started in 2000 as one programmer's catalog of his own favorite music. Electronic music. That's it. It became the database for everything because other collectors showed up and added what they had. Hold onto that. It comes back.

P21. Comics have the exact same problem records do. Maybe worse. Variant covers. Second printings. Newsstand and direct editions. The same title relaunched five times, every one starting over at number one.

P22. And my Coheed books were the perfect example. Claudio Sanchez, the band's singer, writes comics that tell the story behind the albums. I wanted one place that knew exactly what existed, what I had, and what I was missing. (your call: one line, max, on what you were trying to find.) That's a whole episode by itself, and I'm saving it, so that's all you get for now.

## 06-the-weird-stuff · VO over B-ROLL and covers · ~2:30
Content note: Cerebus's later issues are mentioned, not discussed. No values or prices anywhere in this section.

P33. Because then I got to the other pile.

P34. Cerebus. Dave Sim's Cerebus the Aardvark. Self-published through his own company, Aardvark-Vanaheim, for three hundred issues, from 1977 to 2004. It started as a parody of Conan the Barbarian, starring an aardvark, and over the next twenty-six years it turned into something a lot bigger and a lot stranger. It's one of the most important self-published comics ever made. It also has a complicated reputation, especially the later issues, and that's a conversation for another day.

P35. Milk and Cheese, by Evan Dorkin. "Dairy products gone bad." That's the tagline, and it's accurate. A carton of milk and a wedge of cheese who are angry at everything and deal with it through violence. Slave Labor Graphics put it out in the nineties. The first issue is number one. The second issue is "Other Number One." The third is "Third Number One." That's the whole vibe. It's very funny, and extremely nineties.

P36. Scud the Disposable Assassin, by Rob Schrab. Best premise in comics, maybe. In the future, you buy a robot assassin out of a vending machine. It kills its target, then it blows itself up. Scud figures this out, so he doesn't finish the job. He puts his target in the hospital and spends the series taking freelance work to pay the medical bills. It started at Fireman Press in 1994, stopped at issue twenty on a cliffhanger, and Schrab finally finished it at Image in 2008.

P37. And Malibu. Malibu Comics was a nineties publisher with its own superhero universe, the Ultraverse, right up until Marvel bought the company in 1994. (your call: name the Malibu books from the boxes.)

P38. (your call: any book you still can't identify. If there is one, hold it up and say so. That's a great beat.)

P39. And this is where it got humbling.

P40. (Confirm these against your actual experience with Corey's boxes before recording. Cut any that didn't happen to you.) ## 07-what-changed · VO, ending ON CAMERA · ~1:15

P43. So, remember Discogs? One guy's electronic music collection that turned into the catalog for everything?

P44. I'd walked right into the same trap. If I only build for the comics I love, I've built a really nice website for me. A real catalog has to handle what real collectors actually own. And real collections are weird. They have an aardvark in them.

P45. Some of what I found is fixed now. Malibu is searchable. Cerebus is searchable. Some of it isn't. Milk and Cheese is still on the list, and it's staying on the list until it's on the site. (Re-check status on recording day and adjust.)

P46. And honestly, that's the real test. Anybody can find Amazing Spider-Man. The test is whether you can find the strange little book that you and maybe four other people remember buying.

## 08-ending · ON CAMERA · ~0:50

P47. Corey, thank you. For the comics, and for accidentally running the best test this website has ever had.

P48. For me, collecting has always come down to three things. Finding stuff you didn't know existed. Finishing the run. And talking to other people who care about this as much as you do. Discogs nailed that for records. That's what I'm trying to build for comics.

P49. The catalog and the collection part, that works today. The buying and selling part is in beta. You can list a book and people can make you an offer, but there's no checkout yet. You work out the rest with the seller, like the old days. I'm not going to pretend it's further along than it is.
