## 02-the-dark-knight-returns.wav · The Batman before my Batman

Let's start in 1986.

Frank Miller's *The Dark Knight Returns* came out forty years ago, and it's one of those comics that's almost impossible to avoid hearing about if you're a Batman fan.

Four issues. An older Bruce Wayne, retired for ten years, decides Gotham has gotten bad enough that it's time to put the suit back on.

He's not exactly returning to a hero's welcome.

Gotham's overrun with crime, a gang called the Mutants is running wild, and eventually Batman winds up fighting Superman.

Wearing armor, naturally.

[CAM Scene 2, 3:33 to 3:58]
Here's something I probably shouldn't admit. I've been collecting comics since the early 90s, and until recently I didn't even own a copy of The Dark Knight Returns.
I've known about it forever, obviously. I've seen the artwork, I know its reputation, and I've seen its influence all over Batman. But I only just bought it, and I haven't read it yet. So I'm not going to pretend I've got some definitive opinion about it.
[END CAM]

What interests me is how much of its influence you can recognize even without having read the thing.

The older, bulkier Batman. The grimmer approach. That image of Batman standing against Superman in the armor.

It's become part of the character's visual language.

A creator comes along, changes the formula, and if the idea catches on, parts of it become familiar to the next generation of fans.

They might not even know where it started.

Which brings us to my childhood, where Batman wasn't exactly difficult to find.

## 03-batman-returns.wav · Christmas in Gotham

I grew up in Chicagoland in the early 90s, which was a pretty excellent time to be a kid who liked Batman.

We had the Tim Burton movies, the animated series, the toys, the video games. Batman was everywhere.

And I was into all of it.

I remember getting a *Batman Returns* movie book for Christmas. I'm pretty sure it was Michael Singer's official movie book from 1992.

It had all this behind-the-scenes material, photographs, costumes, production designs.

I spent a lot of time looking through it.

I loved the way Gotham looked in those movies.

Burton's version was dark, weird, kind of grotesque. The buildings were enormous, the characters were bizarre, and it didn't look much like any city I'd actually been to.

There was Danny DeVito's Penguin, his whole circus gang, and of course Michelle Pfeiffer as Catwoman.

Who, uh, definitely caught my attention.

And I loved the *Batman Returns* game on Super Nintendo.

That game came out in 1993, and Konami basically turned the movie into a beat-'em-up.

Which was perfect, because the Penguin already had a whole army of clowns and circus freaks running around.

You didn't have to invent an endless supply of disposable enemies. The movie had already done that for you.

You just walked around Gotham beating the shit out of clowns.

I thought that was great.

And of course there was *Batman: The Animated Series*.

Kevin Conroy's Batman, Mark Hamill's Joker, that whole look and feel. It was a huge part of what got me into Batman in the first place.

I still think it's one of the best versions of the character.

So for me, Batman wasn't initially about knowing every comic-book storyline or keeping track of decades of continuity.

It was the movies, the cartoon, the games, and then all this cool-looking stuff I'd encounter in comics.

And one character in particular was practically designed to get my attention.

## 04-azrael.wav · The Batman I couldn't stop drawing

[CAM Scene 3, 5:14 to 5:41]
Man, I loved Azrael when I was a kid. I drew the guy constantly. His original suit, that ridiculous armored Batman suit, all of it.
And the funny thing is I really wasn't following the entire Knightfall story. I didn't fully understand the whole Bane situation. I just knew Batman had this new guy in the suit, and I remember wondering if Bruce Wayne was done being Batman.
But look at this thing. How was a kid in the 90s supposed to see that armor and not want to draw it?
[END CAM]

Everything in the mid-90s had armor and blades and pouches and weird accessories, and some of these guys looked like getting dressed in the morning required a team of engineers.

Azrael fit right in.

Now, knowing the actual story makes all this more interesting.

Jean-Paul Valley is introduced in *Sword of Azrael*, written by Dennis O'Neil with art by Joe Quesada.

He's been conditioned by the Order of St. Dumas, a secretive organization, to serve as an assassin.

Then, during *Knightfall* in 1993, Bane comes along with a pretty clever plan.

Instead of just fighting Batman when he's fresh, he lets Gotham wear him down.

He releases Arkham's inmates, Bruce spends all this time trying to round everybody up, and by the time Bane finally confronts him, Batman is exhausted.

Then Bane breaks his back.

*Batman* #497.

One of the most recognizable Batman covers of the 90s.

And Jean-Paul Valley eventually takes over.

He starts doing things differently. More weapons, more armor, more brutality.

Bruce's replacement turns out to be pretty bad at the whole restraint part of being Batman.

Which is interesting, because as a kid, I was mostly looking at the suit and thinking, yes, more of this please.

And I'm not entirely sure I've changed my mind about the suit.

Something else about this whole period reminds me of the Spider-Man comics of the time.

You had Ben Reilly, the Scarlet Spider, and the Clone Saga, with all this uncertainty about who was really Spider-Man.

Batman gets replaced. Spider-Man gets replaced. Everybody gets a new costume.

It felt like the 90s superhero industry was constantly experimenting with how far it could push these established characters.

Sometimes those decisions worked.

Sometimes you got storylines that took about a thousand issues to explain.

But for a kid who loved drawing superheroes, it was a pretty great time.

And what's funny is that eventually those designs become nostalgic.

You look back at the armored Batman and you remember being excited about something you didn't even fully understand.

Sometimes the imagery grabs you first, and you discover the story behind it years later.

## 05-court-of-owls.wav · The New 52

Fast-forward to 2011.

DC launches the New 52, and Scott Snyder and Greg Capullo take over *Batman*.

[CAM Scene 4, 6:00 to 6:26]
I actually read Court of Owls when it first came out in 2011, and what I loved about it was this idea that Gotham had a secret society operating behind the scenes for generations.
Here's Batman, supposedly the world's greatest detective, discovering that there was this whole other part of his own city, and its history he knew almost nothing about.
That's such a good way to introduce a new villain into a world that's already had decades of stories.
[END CAM]

By this point, Batman had already fought everybody. Joker, Riddler, Two-Face, Penguin, Ra's al Ghul, the list goes on forever.

~ So you make them part of the city's history.

You make them older than Batman.

You make them something that's been there all along, just outside his view.

And Capullo's artwork is fantastic.

There's a sequence in *Batman* #5 where Batman gets trapped in this labyrinth, and the pages actually start changing orientation as he becomes more disoriented.

You have to turn the comic to read it.

I love when artists do that.

It's something that works specifically because you're reading a comic book, rather than watching a movie.

But what I especially appreciate is that Snyder found a way to add something meaningful to Batman's mythology.

Not just another bad guy for him to punch.

An entire piece of Gotham's history that had apparently been hiding there the whole time.

And now we jump ahead to 2024, when Snyder comes back and starts playing with all that familiar Batman history in an entirely different way.

## 06-absolute-batman.wav · Everything old is new again

So let's talk about *Absolute Batman*.

Scott Snyder comes back, this time with Nick Dragotta on art, and they give us this enormous, working-class Batman.

No inherited billions. No Wayne Manor. No Alfred raising Bruce from childhood.

And Batman is built like somebody fed a refrigerator steroids.

I love it.

[CAM Scene 5, 6:35 to 7:12]
I love everything I've read of Absolute Batman so far. I've got some catching up to do, but I love what Snyder and Dragotta are doing, especially the way Snyder rearranges these characters that longtime Batman fans already know.
Bruce's childhood friends include Edward Nygma, Harvey Dent, Oswald Cobblepot, Waylon Jones. If you've been reading Batman for a while, you know exactly who those guys are, but here they're Bruce's friends, and Alfred's relationship to Bruce is completely different too.
I love when comics reward you for knowing some of that old lore while still giving somebody new a way to get into the story.
[END CAM]

~ Riddler. Two-Face. Penguin. Killer Croc.

These are people Bruce traditionally winds up fighting.

The characters are familiar, but all their relationships have changed.

You're recognizing the ingredients, but Snyder is making a completely different meal.

And then there's Alfred.

Traditionally, Alfred Pennyworth is Bruce's butler, guardian, confidant, and basically the guy responsible for making sure Bruce doesn't die of some horrible combination of sleep deprivation and being punched in the head.

He's been there since Bruce was a kid.

In *Absolute Batman*, Alfred's an intelligence operative, and his relationship with Bruce starts under completely different circumstances.

That's a massive change.

And you've got Black Mask.

Roman Sionis, another established Gotham villain, now at the center of this whole Party Animals situation.

And Bane is another good example of how knowing the older comics adds to the fun.

If you know the name Bane, you probably know him as the guy who broke Batman's back.

But knowing what happened during *Knightfall*, and that Bane's victory eventually led to Azrael taking over as Batman, gives you a lot more history to bring into a new interpretation.

You don't have to know any of this to enjoy *Absolute Batman*.

But when you do, there are all these little connections that jump out at you.

~ That's what was fun about talking with Tamu.

Not because he needed me to explain the comic to him. He was already enjoying it.

It was more that I had decades of completely useless Batman trivia rattling around in my head, and suddenly somebody actually wanted to hear it.

It's a rare and beautiful thing.

It reminds me a little of what I love about Jonathan Hickman's X-Men.

With *House of X* and *Powers of X*, Hickman clearly knew the history of these characters, and he used that history to build something that felt completely new.

He didn't just throw everything out.

He took familiar relationships, concepts and bits of lore and found new ways to use them.

That's something I really appreciate as a reader.

When a writer rewards you for paying attention over the years.

And I love that after all these decades, somebody can still take Batman and make him feel new.

Little kids love Batman.

You've got the costume, the cape, the Batmobile, the gadgets.

He's cool before you even know anything about him.

You could spend years reading Batman comics and barely scratch the surface.

Which is why I finally bought *The Dark Knight Returns*.

Forty years late, but I'll get there.

# THREE STANDALONE SHORTS

## short-01-azrael.wav · Batman got replaced in the 90s

When I was a kid in the 90s, Batman suddenly started wearing this insane suit of armor.

And I loved it.

I drew this thing constantly.

But I didn't actually understand the entire story behind it.

This is Jean-Paul Valley, better known as Azrael.

And in 1993, after Bane broke Bruce Wayne's back during *Knightfall*, Jean-Paul eventually took over as Batman.

He already had this awesome costume of his own, with all the blades and armor and everything else you'd expect from a 90s superhero.

Then he became Batman and started adding even more armor and weapons.

Which, of course, made him a pretty terrible Batman.

He was increasingly brutal and didn't have Bruce's sense of restraint.

But I was a kid. I wasn't worrying about any of that.

I was drawing the suit.

And honestly?

Thirty years later, I still think it looks fucking awesome.

I'm Tony from ComixCatalog. Tell me I wasn't the only one.

## short-02-court-of-owls.wav · Batman didn't know his own city

Batman is supposed to know everything about Gotham City.

Every rooftop, every alley, every criminal organization.

So what happens when he discovers a secret society that's been running things behind the scenes for centuries?

That's the idea behind *The Court of Owls*, from Scott Snyder and Greg Capullo's Batman run that started in 2011.

I was reading it when it launched, and I love this story.

Instead of giving Batman one more villain with a costume and a gimmick, Snyder invents an entire organization that's been hiding inside Gotham's history.

And there's this fantastic sequence in *Batman* #5 where Batman's trapped in a labyrinth, and Capullo actually starts rotating the artwork.

You have to turn the comic to follow it.

That's the kind of stuff I love about comics.

It's not just a good Batman story. It's doing something with the medium itself.

And after seventy-something years of Batman comics, these guys still found a way to make Gotham feel mysterious.

I'm Tony from ComixCatalog. If you haven't read *Court of Owls*, put it on your list.

## short-03-absolute-batman.wav · Bruce Wayne's friends are his enemies

One of my favorite things about *Absolute Batman* is how Scott Snyder has rearranged the entire Batman universe.

Look at Bruce Wayne's childhood friends.

Edward Nygma. Harvey Dent. Oswald Cobblepot. Waylon Jones.

If you've been reading Batman comics for years, you probably recognize those names immediately.

Riddler. Two-Face. Penguin. Killer Croc.

Four of Batman's established enemies.

Except in *Absolute Batman*, these guys grew up with Bruce Wayne.

They're his friends.

And that's such a fun way to play with decades of comic-book history.

Even Alfred's relationship with Bruce has been completely reworked.

I was talking about this stuff with a coworker who's twenty years younger than me. We were both reading the series, but he didn't know some of the older lore.

And I love that.

You can enjoy *Absolute Batman* without knowing any of this history.

But if you've been a fan for years, there are all these little connections to discover.

I'm Tony from ComixCatalog, and this is exactly the kind of thing that keeps me reading comics.
